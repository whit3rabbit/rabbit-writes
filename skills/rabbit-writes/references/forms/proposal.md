# Form: proposal

**Register:** `formal`

A form file supplies slots. Only the voice may fill them. Every quoted phrase in this file is under Tells, and every one of them is a phrase to avoid.

A document asking a named reader to approve something: a budget, a design, a piece of work, a change of direction. It differs from a report in what it has to survive. A report has to be correct. A proposal has to be correct and has to answer the objection the reader has not said out loud yet.

That objection is usually one of three: this costs more than it is worth, this has been tried, or somebody else should do it. A proposal that does not name at least the relevant one is asking the reader to raise it themselves, and readers decline rather than argue.

## Slots

- **The ask.** What is being requested, from whom, in what amount, by when. First, and specific enough to approve as written.
- **The problem.** Stated in the reader's terms and with the cost of leaving it alone. A problem with no cost attached is a preference.
- **The proposal.** What will be done. Concrete enough that a reader can tell what they are approving and what they are not.
- **Alternatives considered.** Including doing nothing, and including the one the reader would have suggested. This is the slot that decides whether the document reads as analysis or as advocacy.
- **Cost and timeline.** Ranges with their basis, not point estimates with false precision.
- **Risks, and what happens if it goes wrong.** Named, with whatever reduces each one.
- **What success looks like.** A measurement taken at a date, agreed before the work starts rather than chosen afterwards.
- **Decision requested.** One line, restating the ask, so the last thing read is the thing to act on.

## Bands

| Purpose | Band |
|---|---|
| An internal ask with a known sponsor | 400 to 1,000 words |
| A design or budget proposal | 1,000 to 3,000 words plus an executive summary |
| An external or competitive bid | governed by the request it answers, and the request's structure wins over this one |

Where a client or funder publishes a required structure, that structure replaces these slots outright. A bid scored against a rubric is graded on the rubric.

## Tells

Phrases and shapes named here are the ones to avoid. Nothing in this file is a phrase to use.

- "We are excited to propose."
- "This solution will revolutionize how your team works."
- "Best-in-class" and every unearned superlative in a document whose reader is comparing bids.
- "Minimal disruption to existing workflows" with nothing measuring disruption.
- An alternatives section listing only options that are obviously worse.
- A timeline with no dependencies in it.
- A risk section where every risk is low and every mitigation is monitoring.
- A cost figure with no range and no basis, which reads as precise and is not.

## What the mechanical layer sees here

The `formal` extra-strict cells run, and this is the form where they earn the most. Promotional language is the native failure mode of a document written to persuade, and significance inflation is what a proposal reaches for when the evidence is thin.

That does not make persuasion a defect. A proposal is allowed to advocate. What the extra-strict reading catches is advocacy that has replaced the specifics rather than resting on them, which the portability test settles faster than any rule: a sentence that would be just as true of somebody else's proposal is filler in yours.

Length usually clears the reliability floor. The paragraph-spread number is worth reading here, because a proposal assembled from a template tends to have uniform sections, and uniform sections are what a reader skims.
