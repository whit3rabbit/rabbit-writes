# Form: whitepaper

**Register:** `formal`

A form file supplies slots. Only the voice may fill them. Every quoted phrase in this file is under Tells, and every one of them is a phrase to avoid.

A published document that argues a position with evidence, from an organization rather than a person, to a reader who is evaluating both the argument and the organization behind it. That double reading is the form's whole difficulty. A whitepaper is marketing that has agreed to be checkable, and a reader who catches it being marketing stops checking.

The line to hold: a whitepaper may argue that a problem matters and that a class of approach solves it. The moment it argues that one product solves it, it is a brochure with citations, and readers who know the genre are looking for exactly that turn.

## Slots

- **Title and subtitle.** The claim, then its scope.
- **Abstract or summary.** What the paper argues and what it rests on, in a paragraph.
- **The problem.** Sized with somebody else's numbers where possible. A problem sized only by the organization publishing the paper is the first thing a skeptical reader discounts.
- **Why current approaches fall short.** Fairly, including what they do well. An approach knocked down in its weakest form takes the paper's credibility with it.
- **The approach.** Described at the level of mechanism, not brand. A reader must be able to evaluate it independently of who is selling it.
- **Evidence.** Measurements, deployments, or a worked case. Sources cited so a reader can follow them.
- **Limits.** Where the approach does not apply. The slot that separates the form from the brochure, and the one most often missing.
- **References.** A citation style applies here. `references/citations/` has the four.

## Bands

| Purpose | Band |
|---|---|
| A position piece | 1,500 to 3,000 words |
| A full technical whitepaper | 3,000 to 8,000 words with figures and references |
| Past 8,000 words | it is a report, and calling it a whitepaper is a distribution decision |

## Tells

Phrases and shapes named here are the ones to avoid. Nothing in this file is a phrase to use.

- "In today's rapidly evolving threat landscape."
- "Organizations must adapt or risk being left behind."
- "Our unique approach" in a section that promised to describe a mechanism.
- "Studies show" with no study named.
- A market-size figure from the publisher's own marketing department, cited as though it were external.
- A comparison table where the publisher's column is the only one with every row filled.
- A conclusion that becomes a product page.
- A statistic with no year on it, which is how a five-year-old number keeps circulating.

## What the mechanical layer sees here

The `formal` extra-strict cells all point at this form's failure mode. Promotional language, significance inflation, boilerplate clusters, and future-narrative closers are the four moves a whitepaper makes when its evidence runs out, and the extra-strict reading is the right one here even though it will flag sentences the author considers legitimate framing.

The portability test settles the arguments the rules cannot: a paragraph about the problem that would be just as true in a competitor's whitepaper is not evidence, it is throat-clearing that both companies wrote.

Length clears the reliability floor, so the stylometrics apply. Watch paragraph spread in particular. A whitepaper assembled section by section, often by several people, tends toward uniform blocks, and uniformity is the structural signal that survives every vocabulary fix.
